I can still give you directions to a house that no longer exists. Left at
the second light, past the church with the blue door, three houses down
from the one with the crooked mailbox — except the church repainted its
door a decade ago, the crooked mailbox was replaced by someone with less
patience for character than its previous owner, and the house itself was
demolished nine years back to make room for a parking structure that
serves a hospital annex nobody in my family has ever had reason to visit.
None of that touches the directions. The directions still work, in the
sense that if you followed them today you would arrive at the correct
coordinates. You would just find, once you got there, that the thing the
directions were pointing at had quietly stopped being true.

This is, I think, most of what memory of place actually is: a set of
directions that still parse, aimed at a target that has moved or
vanished. I don't experience the loss of the house as a single grief,
the way you might grieve a person. It's smaller and stranger than that —
a low, recurring dissonance every time the directions fire and the
destination doesn't answer. I'll be standing in an unrelated hallway,
smell something like the specific mildew of our old basement, and for
half a second the directions activate: down the stairs, past the water
heater, to the shelf where the good flashlight lived. There is no
basement to walk into. The directions run anyway, like a phone number
you can still recite for a house whose phone was disconnected decades
ago.

## What the parking structure can't take

I went back once, after the demolition, mostly to see what a childhood
looks like with its central location removed. The parking structure is
unremarkable — six levels, the smell of exhaust and hot concrete, a
ticket machine that didn't take the bill I tried to feed it. I stood on
what I calculated to be roughly the second floor of the old house,
plus or minus the width of a bedroom, and felt considerably less than I
expected to feel. This surprised me at the time. I had built up the visit
as a kind of reckoning, and the reckoning refused to arrive on schedule.

What I understand now, a few years further out, is that the house was
never really stored in the coordinates. It was stored in the directions —
the sequence of turns, the church, the mailbox, the specific slant of
afternoon light through a window that faced the wrong way for good light
and got it anyway, some mornings, for about eleven minutes. Demolishing
the building didn't touch any of that sequence. You cannot tear down a
set of directions with a wrecking ball. You can only make them
inaccurate, which is a different and in some ways more durable kind of
damage — not an absence but a persistent, low-grade wrongness, a map that
keeps confidently pointing at a place that will no longer receive you.

I've started, lately, writing the directions down properly, before they
degrade any further. Not the geography — the geography is a matter of
public record, findable on any map that still bothers to note the
hospital annex — but the texture of the walk itself: which crack in the
sidewalk you had to step over without looking, which neighbor's dog
barked at exactly this point and no other, where the smell of somebody's
dinner would reliably reach you two houses before their porch light did.
This is the part that dies fastest and matters most, and no parking
structure has any power over it, because it was never really about the
house. It was about the specific, unrepeatable act of arriving there, on
foot, as a particular child, on however many thousand ordinary afternoons
that added up, eventually, to something worth still being able to
describe.

The house is gone. The directions, it turns out, were never really about
the house at all.
