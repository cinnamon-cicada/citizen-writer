I write with a window open, even in the months when it costs something to
keep it that way. Not because the air in the room is bad — it isn't,
particularly — but because a closed window turns a room into a diagram of
itself: four walls, a desk, a person trying to have thoughts in isolation
from everything that produced them. An open window keeps the argument
honest. It lets in the delivery truck's reverse alarm, the neighbor's
radio tuned to a station I've never identified, two kids negotiating the
terms of a game I can't quite follow. None of it is material, exactly. All
of it is context.

I used to think the ideal writing environment was a kind of vacuum —
silence, blank walls, nothing to distract the sentence from arriving
whole. I built one once, in a rented room with the shades always down,
and wrote there for the better part of a year. The sentences that came out
of that room were competent and airless. They had the flatness of food
cooked without salt. I could not, at the time, explain why; I only noticed
that I read them back and felt nothing, the way you feel nothing reading a
manual for an appliance you don't own.

It took leaving that room to understand what had gone missing. A sentence
written in total isolation has no one to be polite to, no ambient noise to
argue against, no accidental music to borrow a rhythm from. It is
unaccountable. The window fixes that by force. When a car alarm cuts
through a sentence I am building, I have two choices — abandon the
sentence, or build it strong enough to survive the interruption. The
second option turns out to be the more interesting discipline. A sentence
that can hold its shape against a car alarm is usually a sentence that can
hold its shape against a reader's boredom, too, which is the harder and
more likely threat.

There is also the plainer fact that the window is where the material
comes from. Not in the sense of note-taking — I am a poor note-taker, and
most of what drifts in through the glass is gone within the hour, the way
dreams are gone by the time you've found a pen. It matters anyway, the way
weather matters to a garden it never directly touches. The kids
negotiating their game outside are working out, in real time, the same
problem I am usually working out on the page: how much can be assumed,
how much has to be stated, what happens when one party changes the rules
mid-sentence without telling the other. I don't write any of this down.
I just notice that my own sentences get a little more honest about
negotiation and revision after twenty minutes of listening to it happen
one floor below.

## The cost of the open window

None of this is free. An open window lets in cold and heat along with
noise, and there are days the essay I meant to write is simply defeated by
a jackhammer two lots over, or by August doing what August does to a room
with no air conditioning. I have abandoned entire afternoons this way. I
don't think of them as lost, exactly — more like tuition. You don't get
the street's honesty without occasionally paying its price, and a writer
who only works in perfect conditions is training herself for a kind of
weather that doesn't actually exist in most writing lives.

What the window really offers, I think, is a reminder that the essay
isn't happening in a clean room somewhere above the world, taking notes on
it from a safe altitude. It's happening in a specific room, on a specific
street, at a specific and slightly too-loud hour, and the sentences that
survive that context tend to be the ones that were built to survive
something. I don't always love the noise. I have come to trust it more
than silence, which is a different thing, and slower to admit.

So the window stays open, weather permitting and sometimes not quite
permitting. The street keeps talking. I keep half-listening, and writing
down maybe one sentence in twenty that the afternoon hands me, which is
about the ratio you'd expect from any conversation you didn't choose to
have but couldn't, in the end, regret having overheard.
