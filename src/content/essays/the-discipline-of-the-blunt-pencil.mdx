There is a pencil on my desk that I have sharpened exactly once. I bought
it new, took the point off within an hour of ordinary use, and have not
touched the sharpener since. This was not laziness, or not only laziness.
It was an experiment that turned into a habit that turned, eventually,
into something I would defend at length to anyone unwise enough to ask
about my process. The experiment was this: what happens to a first draft
when the tool that produces it is incapable of precision?

A sharp pencil promises exactness it cannot yet deliver. Draft sentences
are not exact; they are approximations of a thought that hasn't finished
happening. A sharp point encourages you to write as though the thought
were already settled — clean letters, confident margins, the visual
appearance of a decision that hasn't actually been made. The blunt pencil
can't keep that promise, and so it doesn't make it. Its line is thick,
slightly uncertain at the edges, obviously the product of a hand still
working something out. Looking down at a page of blunt-pencil handwriting,
you cannot mistake it for a finished thing. It looks like what it is: a
draft, still warm, not yet asked to be right.

I think this matters more than it sounds like it should. Writers ruin a
striking number of good first drafts by holding them to the standard of
the sentence they'll eventually become. The blunt pencil is a small,
physical way of refusing that standard before it has a chance to take
hold. You cannot write a beautifully precise sentence with a point that
wide, so you stop trying, and something looser and more honest comes out
instead — closer to speech, closer to the actual shape of the thought
before it got tidied.

## What gets lost, and what doesn't

I don't want to overclaim for a piece of graphite. The blunt pencil
doesn't generate ideas; it just refuses to let you dress an idea up before
it's ready to be seen in public. The actual thinking still has to happen,
usually badly, usually more than once. What the pencil changes is the
temptation to stop early — to mistake a clean-looking sentence for a
finished one, simply because it photographed well on the page.

There's a second, quieter benefit, which is that a blunt pencil forces
larger handwriting, and larger handwriting forces fewer words per line,
and fewer words per line forces you to notice, physically, how long a
sentence has gotten before your eye would otherwise have skated past its
length. I have caught more run-on sentences by the visual bulk of their
blunt-pencil handwriting than I ever caught by reading them aloud. The
tool does some of the editor's job before the editor has been called in.

None of this survives contact with revision, which is the point. Once a
draft exists — messy, thick-lined, unmistakably provisional — it gets
typed up, and the typing is where the sharpening happens: sentences get
narrowed, qualified, cut in half, occasionally cut entirely. The blunt
pencil's job was never to produce good prose. It was to produce prose that
knew it wasn't good yet, so that the writer wouldn't be fooled into
protecting it. A sharp pencil, used too early, produces sentences that
feel finished and therefore feel worth defending. A blunt one produces
sentences that feel like scaffolding, which is exactly what a first draft
should feel like, and which makes them so much easier to tear down later
without grief.

I have tried, a few times, to go back to sharp pencils for early drafts,
usually because the blunt one has finally worn down past even its own low
standard of usefulness. It never lasts. Within a page or two the
sentences start arriving too clean, too soon, and I find myself protecting
lines that haven't earned protection yet. So the pencil goes back in the
drawer unsharpened, or a new one gets bought and immediately, deliberately,
worn down. It is a small and slightly absurd ritual. It has also, more
than any advice I've read about revision, kept me from falling in love
with my own first drafts before they had done anything to deserve it.
